A Peak No Footstep Has Touched

Why No One Has Been Able to Climb Mount Kailash

At 6,638 meters, it is far from the tallest mountain in Asia — yet it remains the only major peak on Earth that has never recorded a single summit.

Everest has been summited more than 7,000 times. K2, Denali, Kilimanjaro — all conquered, mapped, and photographed from the top. Mount Kailash, rising over the Tibetan plateau at a height thousands of climbers could technically handle, has never once been reached. Not because it can't be done. Because, again and again, the people who could have done it chose to walk away.

01 — Sacred Ground

A Mountain Kailash Sacred to a Quarter of Humanity

Mount Kailash sits at the spiritual center of four faiths simultaneously — Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and the Bön tradition native to Tibet. For Hindus, it is the eternal seat of Lord Shiva. For Buddhists, it is the axis of the universe itself, the point around which all existence turns. For Jains, it marks the site where their first spiritual teacher achieved liberation. For followers of Bön, it is the soul-mountain of the entire Tibetan landscape.

The Kora: Walking Instead of Climbing

Each year, thousands of pilgrims travel days across the plateau not to climb Kailash, but to circle it. The 52-kilometer route around its base, known as the kora, is considered one of the most powerful acts of devotion a person can perform. Some pilgrims walk it upright in a matter of days; others complete the entire circuit through full-body prostrations, lying down and rising again for every length of their own body, a journey that can take several weeks in freezing, oxygen-thin air.

Why Walking Around Beats Walking Up

Buddhist tradition holds that a single kora clears the accumulated sin of one lifetime, and that 108 circuits can bring liberation itself. Within that belief system, standing on the summit isn't a greater achievement than the kora — it's a violation of it. To plant a flag on Kailash would mean placing a foot above the very deity or cosmic axis the pilgrims have traveled thousands of miles to honor from below.

02 — The Climbers Who Said No

Great Mountaineers Who Turned Down the Chance

What makes Kailash unusual isn't just faith — it's that the world's most accomplished climbers have actively chosen not to challenge it, even when given the opportunity.

If we conquer this mountain, then we conquer something in people's souls. Reinhold Messner, on declining permission to climb Kailash

In the 1980s, the Chinese government granted formal climbing permission to Reinhold Messner, the Italian mountaineer who had already made the first solo, oxygen-free ascent of Everest. He never made the attempt. Years later, asked about a Spanish team's plan to try the same route, he suggested they climb something harder instead, since Kailash was neither especially tall nor especially difficult by his standards. The backlash from mountaineers around the world was strong enough that the Spanish team abandoned the plan entirely.

Sir Edmund Hillary, the first person to summit Everest, also publicly declined any attempt on Kailash, citing the same respect for its sacred meaning. Between them, two of the most celebrated names in mountaineering history looked at a peak well within their ability — and chose to leave it untouched.

03 — A History of Retreat

What Happened to the People Who Tried to Climb Mount Kailash

Kailash has seen far fewer serious attempts than its fame implies, and every documented one ended the same way: turned back, called off, or abandoned before it truly began.

  • 1926 British mountaineer Hugh Ruttledge studied the mountain's north face and concluded it was simply unclimbable. Around the same time, explorer Colonel R. C. Wilson believed he had located a viable route up the southern face, but heavy snowfall moved in before he could act on it.
  • 1936 Austrian climber Herbert Tichy arrived hoping to become the first person to summit Kailash. Before setting out, he asked a local lama whether the climb was possible. The lama told him that only someone entirely free of sin could climb the mountain — and that such a person wouldn't need ropes or a route, since they could simply turn into a bird and fly to the summit. Tichy took it as his answer and turned back.
  • 1980s Reinhold Messner became the only climber ever formally granted permission by Chinese authorities to attempt the summit. He chose not to make the climb.
  • 2001 A Spanish team requested and began planning permission to attempt Kailash. International protest from climbers and pilgrims led them to abandon the plan before it started.

The Unverified Attempts

Beyond these documented cases, the record goes quiet. Local oral traditions mention travelers who supposedly tried to climb Kailash without permission, with stories ranging from sudden whiteouts to unexplained illness partway up. None of these accounts have ever been officially confirmed — no equipment, no remains, no verified route has ever surfaced. What remains is a near-century-long pattern: every known attempt on Kailash ended not in an accident, but in a decision to stop.

04 — Beyond Belief

Is It Actually Difficult to Climb Mount Kailash — or Just Forbidden?

Faith aside, Kailash isn't an easy peak by any measure. Its four sheer faces are made of loose, crumbling rock rather than solid granite, its walls run close to vertical in several sections, and weather on the Tibetan plateau can shift from clear skies to whiteout conditions within the hour. Its remote location, deep in one of the least accessible parts of western Tibet, adds days of difficult travel before a climber would even reach its base.

The Legal Barrier

Even so, the deciding factor has rarely been physical. The Chinese government, which administers the region, does not issue climbing permits for Kailash under any ordinary circumstance — the one known exception being Messner's offer in the 1980s. Local and national authorities treat the restriction as a matter of protecting the beliefs of the millions of pilgrims who travel there each year, not as a response to any technical failure.

Put the two together, and you get a mountain that is watched over by governments, protected by faith, and left alone by the very climbers capable of reaching its top.

05 — The Lasting Enigma

Why Mount Kailash Stays a Mystery

Part of what keeps Kailash so compelling is that it has never been "solved" the way most great peaks eventually are. There is no summit photograph to demystify it, no published route beta, no GPS track reducing it to a known quantity. It remains exactly what it has always been: a four-sided pyramid of dark rock and snow, visible for miles across the plateau, that no one has ever stood on top of.

Whether or not a reader holds any of the beliefs built around it, there is something rare about a place in the modern world that has been left alone — not because reaching it is impossible, but because, time and again, people have agreed not to try.

06 — The Strange Geometry

The Unusual Shape and Setting of Kailash

Part of what draws attention to Kailash long before anyone discusses climbing it is simply how it looks. Unlike the jagged, irregular ridgelines typical of the surrounding Transhimalaya range, Kailash rises as a near-symmetrical, four-sided formation, with distinct faces oriented roughly toward the cardinal directions. From a distance across the plateau, that regularity is striking enough that early travelers described it less as a mountain among mountains and more as a single monument set apart from everything around it.

Geology, Not Just Geometry

Geologists who have studied the region attribute Kailash's form to conglomerate rock — layers of gravel and sediment cemented together over millions of years and later uplifted by the same tectonic forces that raised the Himalaya. The horizontal banding visible on its faces comes from these sedimentary layers, and the sharper edges result from how that conglomerate rock fractures and erodes compared to the granite and gneiss found in neighboring peaks. None of this is a settled controversy in mainstream geology; it is offered here as the accepted explanation, alongside the fact that the mountain's symmetry has long invited speculation, folklore, and fringe theories that go well beyond what the geological record supports.

A Compass Point for the Region

Mount Kailash also sits near the source of four major Asian rivers — the Indus, the Sutlej, the Brahmaputra (known locally as the Yarlung Tsangpo), and the Karnali, a principal tributary of the Ganges. In several regional traditions, this is read as further confirmation of the mountain's role as a geographic and spiritual axis, feeding the water that sustains hundreds of millions of people living far beyond the plateau itself.

07 — The Lakes Below

Manasarovar and Rakshastal: The Twin Lakes at Kailash's Feet

Most pilgrimages to Kailash begin, not end, at a pair of high-altitude lakes resting at its base: Lake Manasarovar and Lake Rakshastal. The two sit only a few kilometers apart, yet tradition treats them as near opposites.

Manasarovar, the Lake of the Mind

Manasarovar is regarded as one of the holiest bodies of water in both Hindu and Buddhist tradition, believed to have been formed in the mind of Brahma and considered pure enough that a single bath in its waters is said to cleanse the sins of many lifetimes. Its glassy, freshwater surface reflects Kailash on clear mornings, and most pilgrimage itineraries build in a full day here for ritual bathing and circumambulation before the kora itself begins.

Rakshastal, the Lake of Shadows

A short distance west, Rakshastal is a saltwater lake traditionally associated with negative or demonic forces in regional folklore, and it draws comparatively few visitors and no ritual bathing. Geographically the two lakes are connected by a narrow channel, and their contrast — one fresh and revered, one saline and avoided — is often cited by pilgrims as a physical echo of the balance the entire Kailash region is believed to represent.

08 — Visiting, Not Climbing

Planning a Kailash Pilgrimage: Permits, Season, and the Kora Itself

While no one climbs Kailash, a great many people visit it, and the practical realities of doing so are almost as demanding as a mountaineering expedition — just aimed at the base rather than the summit.

What is the best Time For Kailash Masnarovar Yatra

The Kailash Mansarovar Tour season generally runs from May through September, when the high passes are clear of the heaviest snow. Late spring and early autumn tend to bring the most stable weather, while the Tibetan monsoon months of July and August can bring sudden rain and cloud cover over the plateau. Outside this window, snow and extreme cold close the region to most travelers entirely.

Permits and Access

Foreign visitors need a Tibet Travel Permit in addition to a Chinese visa, and independent travel is not permitted — visits must be arranged through a licensed agency with a registered guide, typically as part of an organized group. Indian pilgrims can alternatively join the government-coordinated Kailash Manasarovar Yatra, which uses designated overland or, in some years, partial-flight routes depending on diplomatic and logistical arrangements in place that season. Because routes and requirements shift with regional conditions, it is worth confirming current permit rules directly with a licensed operator before booking.

What the Kora Actually Demands

The 52-kilometer circuit is usually walked over three days, with most itineraries overnighting at basic guesthouses near Dirapuk and Zutrulpuk monasteries. The second day is the hardest, crossing the Dolma La Pass at roughly 5,630 meters — higher than Everest Base Camp — before descending steeply on the far side. Altitude sickness is the most common reason pilgrims turn back, which is why most operators recommend several days of acclimatization at Lhasa or Manasarovar before starting the walk, along with a slow, well-paced ascent rather than rushing the circuit.

A Practical Note

Nothing about visiting Kailash requires technical climbing skill or equipment — it is a high-altitude trek, not a mountaineering route. The real preparation is physical conditioning for sustained walking at extreme elevation, appropriate cold-weather gear, and enough acclimatization time to cross Dolma La safely. Travelers with pre-existing heart or respiratory conditions are generally advised to consult a doctor before attempting the kora, given the altitude involved.

A mix of reasons keeps Kailash unclimbed: deep religious significance to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and Bön followers who consider it sacred ground; a lack of climbing permits from the Chinese government, which administers the region; and voluntary refusal by top mountaineers, including Reinhold Messner and Sir Edmund Hillary, who were offered chances to climb it but declined out of respect for its spiritual meaning. The mountain's genuinely difficult terrain and harsh weather add to the challenge, but the primary barrier has always been cultural and legal rather than physical impossibility.

Commercial and private flights don't operate directly over the mountain itself. Overflights of this remote part of the Tibetan plateau are heavily restricted by Chinese aviation authorities, and the area sits far from established commercial flight paths in any case. Visitors typically reach the region by road from Lhasa or nearby Tibetan towns rather than by air, and the closest airports remain a significant drive from the mountain.
